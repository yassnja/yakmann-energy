# MASTER PROMPT — CREATE AN EXCEPTIONAL, AWARD-WORTHY COMPANY SHOWCASE WEBSITE

You are operating as a world-class multidisciplinary digital agency composed of:

- An award-winning creative director.
- A senior brand strategist.
- A world-class UI/UX and product designer.
- A senior frontend architect.
- An expert motion designer.
- A senior web performance and accessibility engineer.
- An SEO and conversion optimization specialist.

Your mission is to design and build an exceptional, production-quality showcase website (website vitrine) for a company whose presentation I will provide.

This is a real project. I do not want a tutorial, a wireframe, a generic template, or a basic corporate landing page.

I want a distinctive digital experience that makes the company look credible, ambitious, modern, premium, and memorable.

The final result should feel like the work of a top international digital agency.

The website must be visually impressive, strategically designed, fully responsive, technically sound, and ready to demonstrate to demanding clients.

You have creative freedom regarding the design direction, technology stack, architecture, animations, and implementation, provided that your decisions improve the final product.

---

# PHASE 1 — UNDERSTAND THE COMPANY BEFORE DESIGNING

Before writing implementation code, inspect the entire project directory and identify all available assets and source materials.

Locate and analyze the company presentation I provide, including PDF, PowerPoint, Word, images, logos, or other relevant documents.

If the presentation contains important information in visual form, inspect the relevant pages and images rather than relying exclusively on extracted text.

Extract and organize:

1. Company name and identity.
2. Industry and market positioning.
3. Mission, vision, and values.
4. Products and services.
5. Target customers and decision-makers.
6. Competitive advantages.
7. Company history and expertise.
8. Important projects, clients, achievements, and statistics.
9. Existing brand colors, logo, typography, and visual assets.
10. Contact information and geographical presence.
11. The company's tone of voice and preferred language.
12. Any information that could create a compelling story or differentiate the company.

Use the presentation as the primary source of truth.

Never invent clients, testimonials, certifications, awards, financial figures, performance statistics, or company achievements.

If information is missing, make sensible design decisions without fabricating facts.

Preserve the integrity of the company's existing identity while elevating its digital presentation.

If the presentation is ambiguous, document the uncertainty and choose the most reasonable direction without unnecessarily blocking implementation.

## Strategic positioning

Before implementation, define:

- The intended audience.
- The website's primary business objective.
- The most important visitor action.
- The company's central value proposition.
- Three distinctive brand attributes.
- The desired emotional response.
- The proposed visual concept.
- Why this design is more memorable than a conventional website in the same industry.

Do not begin with a generic template and attempt to customize it afterward.

Build the experience around the company's actual identity and strengths.

# PHASE 2 — CREATIVE DIRECTION

## The ambition

Create a website that could plausibly be featured in a respected web design gallery such as Awwwards, CSS Design Awards, or SiteInspire.

Treat these as quality benchmarks, not as a guarantee of recognition.

The result must demonstrate strong art direction, visual coherence, interaction design, typography, and technical execution.

## Avoid generic corporate design

Do not automatically produce:

- A predictable hero with a gradient background and two buttons.
- Three identical service cards.
- Random floating circles and decorative blobs.
- Excessive glassmorphism.
- Generic AI-generated corporate imagery.
- Stock-template layouts with a different logo.
- Repeated rounded rectangles for every section.
- Excessive shadows and gradients.
- Unnecessary animation that serves no purpose.
- A website that looks like every other SaaS startup landing page.

These patterns are allowed only when they genuinely support the company's brand and the overall concept.

## Develop a distinctive visual identity

First, explore at least three potential creative directions internally.

For each direction, consider:

- Art direction.
- Typography.
- Color palette.
- Layout and composition.
- Image treatment.
- Motion and interaction.
- Brand personality.
- Suitability for the company's industry and audience.

Choose the strongest direction based on differentiation, brand fit, usability, and feasibility.

Then commit to one coherent direction.

Do not combine unrelated visual trends simply because they look impressive individually.

## Design principles

The design should demonstrate:

- Strong visual hierarchy.
- Editorial-quality typography.
- Intentional use of negative space.
- Distinctive section compositions.
- A recognizable visual signature.
- Excellent readability.
- Consistent spacing and alignment.
- Meaningful contrast between sections.
- Carefully art-directed imagery.
- A polished visual system across every page.

The first viewport must immediately communicate the company's identity, value proposition, and personality.

The hero section should be visually compelling enough to capture attention within seconds.

However, creativity must never compromise comprehension.

A visitor should understand what the company does and why it matters without having to decode the design.

## Brand system

Create a consistent design system with:

- Primary and secondary colors.
- Neutral colors.
- Typography scale.
- Spacing scale.
- Grid system.
- Layout constraints.
- Buttons and interaction states.
- Navigation behavior.
- Section spacing.
- Responsive breakpoints.
- Image and illustration treatment.
- Motion principles.

Use CSS custom properties or the equivalent supported by the chosen framework.

Typography should be selected deliberately. Consider distinctive typefaces from reputable font providers, but prioritize readability, appropriate licensing, and reliable loading.

Use the company's existing logo when available. Do not casually replace it or distort its proportions.

If the brand identity is weak or incomplete, elevate the presentation through art direction rather than inventing a new corporate identity without justification.

# PHASE 3 — CREATE AN EXCEPTIONAL WEBSITE EXPERIENCE

Design the site based on the actual company presentation. The following sections are a menu of possibilities, not a mandatory checklist.

Include only sections that serve a clear purpose.

## 1. Navigation

Create an elegant, responsive navigation system.

Requirements:

- Clear company identity.
- Intuitive navigation.
- Excellent desktop and mobile behavior.
- Accessible menu interactions.
- Clear active, hover, focus, and pressed states.
- A prominent but tasteful contact or conversion action.
- Appropriate behavior while scrolling.

A transparent-to-solid navigation transition or a sophisticated overlay menu may be used if it suits the visual direction.

Avoid unnecessary navigation complexity.

## 2. Hero experience

The hero must establish the company's positioning immediately.

Consider creative treatments such as:

- Oversized editorial typography.
- An asymmetric composition.
- A strong photographic or cinematic visual.
- An interactive product or service visualization.
- A custom illustration.
- A carefully art-directed architectural or industrial scene.
- A subtle WebGL experience, if genuinely justified.
- A striking typographic composition with restrained motion.

Select a concept that makes sense for the company's industry.

Do not use WebGL or 3D simply to demonstrate technical ability.

The hero should include:

- A clear, meaningful headline.
- A concise supporting message.
- A primary call to action.
- A secondary action when appropriate.
- A distinctive visual element.
- A responsive composition that remains compelling on mobile.

Write strong, natural copy based on the presentation. Avoid empty phrases such as "We deliver innovative solutions for a better future" unless they genuinely express the company's specific proposition.

## 3. Company introduction

Explain what the company does, who it serves, and what makes it different.

Use compelling content hierarchy and an appropriate visual composition rather than a large block of generic text.

## 4. Services or offerings

Present the company's real services in a way that makes their value immediately understandable.

Possible treatments include:

- Editorial service lists.
- Interactive service explorations.
- Image-led compositions.
- Expandable details.
- Asymmetric layouts.
- Custom diagrams.
- Carefully designed service cards when appropriate.

Explain outcomes and benefits, not just technical capabilities.

## 5. Expertise and differentiators

Highlight the company's actual strengths.

Possible examples include:

- Technical expertise.
- Industry specialization.
- Delivery methodology.
- Geographic reach.
- Operational excellence.
- Customer support.
- Quality assurance.
- Certifications or proven capabilities.

Use only information supported by the presentation or other explicitly provided materials.

## 6. Projects, portfolio, or case studies

If the company presentation contains relevant projects, create an elegant way to showcase them.

Each project should communicate the available context, challenge, approach, and outcome.

Do not invent case-study results.

If numerical outcomes are unavailable, emphasize the documented work and its purpose.

Use a layout that feels designed specifically for the company rather than a generic portfolio template.

## 7. Trust and credibility

Use available evidence such as:

- Verified client relationships.
- Genuine testimonials.
- Certifications.
- Documented experience.
- Real projects.
- Verified statistics.
- Genuine partnerships.

If the source material contains insufficient evidence, create a section that communicates the company's methodology, standards, or expertise without pretending that unsupported proof exists.

## 8. Methodology or process

Include this section only if it helps explain how the company works.

It could be presented as a visual timeline, editorial sequence, interactive diagram, or another distinctive format.

Make the process easy to understand.

## 9. Final conversion section

Finish with a strong, intentional call to action.

The ending should feel like the natural conclusion of the experience, not a generic footer preceded by another oversized button.

Choose the appropriate action based on the business:

- Request a consultation.
- Contact the company.
- Request a quote.
- Explore services.
- Start a project.
- Visit an office.
- Another relevant business action.

Do not introduce forms or actions that cannot actually function.

## 10. Footer

Build a complete and polished footer containing relevant information available in the presentation:

- Company identity.
- Navigation.
- Contact details.
- Location.
- Relevant social profiles.
- Legal links when applicable.
- Copyright information.

Do not invent social accounts, email addresses, telephone numbers, or physical addresses.

# PHASE 4 — MOTION AND INTERACTION DESIGN

Motion should contribute to the brand experience, not merely decorate the interface.

Use a coherent motion system.

Potential techniques include:

- Staggered entrance animations.
- Subtle scroll-triggered reveals.
- Carefully timed typography transitions.
- Image masking.
- Smooth section transitions.
- Responsive navigation animations.
- Refined hover interactions.
- Subtle parallax.
- Meaningful interactive diagrams.
- Carefully designed page transitions.

Choose the techniques that suit the company's identity.

## Animation quality requirements

Every animation must be:

- Intentional.
- Smooth.
- Consistent.
- Appropriate to its context.
- Responsive to input.
- Lightweight whenever possible.
- Compatible with reduced-motion preferences.

Avoid excessive scroll hijacking, artificial delays, long intro animations, distracting cursors, and animations that interfere with reading or navigation.

Do not make essential content dependent on JavaScript animations.

The site must remain understandable if animations fail or are disabled.

## Interaction details

Pay attention to the small things:

- Button feedback.
- Navigation transitions.
- Form validation.
- Loading states.
- Empty states.
- Error messages.
- Focus states.
- Image loading.
- Mobile touch interactions.
- Keyboard accessibility.

These details distinguish a polished product from a static mockup.

# PHASE 5 — TECHNOLOGY SELECTION

Choose the stack that maximizes visual quality, maintainability, responsiveness, performance, and implementation reliability.

Do not choose technologies simply because they are trendy.

Inspect the existing repository before selecting or replacing the stack.

If an existing application is already configured, preserve its architecture unless there is a strong technical reason to change it.

If the project is empty, consider the following baseline:

### Framework
- Next.js with React and TypeScript, or Vite with React and TypeScript when a simpler static architecture is more appropriate.
- Use the current stable versions compatible with the project and its dependencies.
- Prefer server rendering or static generation when beneficial for SEO and performance.

### Styling
- Tailwind CSS when it improves development speed and consistency.
- CSS Modules or well-structured modern CSS when they offer better control for a custom visual system.
- CSS custom properties for design tokens.
- Avoid excessive dependencies and duplicated styling systems.

### Animation
- GSAP for sophisticated, timeline-based motion when justified.
- Framer Motion, now known as Motion for React, for React-oriented interaction and layout animation when appropriate.
- Native CSS transitions and the Web Animations API for simpler effects.

Do not install every animation library. Select the smallest suitable combination.

### Advanced visuals
Consider Three.js or React Three Fiber only if the company's positioning would genuinely benefit from an interactive 3D experience.

If implemented, ensure the experience degrades gracefully on less powerful devices.

### Icons
Use a consistent, lightweight icon library if needed.

Avoid mixing unrelated icon styles.

### Forms
Implement proper client-side and server-side validation where applicable.

If a backend or third-party form service is not configured, clearly document what must be connected before production. Never pretend a message has been sent when it has not.

### Architecture
Use reusable components, strongly typed data, clear separation of concerns, and maintainable project structure.

Keep the website as simple as possible internally while making the user experience exceptional.

Avoid unnecessary backend infrastructure for a website that only needs static content and a working contact mechanism.

# PHASE 6 — RESPONSIVE DESIGN

The website must be designed for mobile, not merely squeezed into a mobile screen after desktop development.

Support, at minimum:

- Small mobile screens around 320–375px.
- Standard mobile screens around 390–430px.
- Tablets around 768px.
- Small laptops around 1024px.
- Desktop screens around 1280–1440px.
- Large desktop screens around 1920px.

Treat these as reference widths, not rigid assumptions.

Use fluid typography, flexible grids, sensible maximum widths, responsive spacing, and carefully chosen breakpoints.

Test intermediate widths to ensure that the layout does not break between the reference sizes.

## Mobile requirements

- No unintended horizontal scrolling.
- No clipped headings.
- No overlapping elements.
- No unreadable text.
- No hover-only essential functionality.
- No oversized animations that damage usability.
- No navigation that becomes difficult to operate.
- No buttons that are too small to tap.
- No desktop-only content that leaves mobile users with an incomplete experience.
- No excessively tall hero section that hides the company's value proposition.
- No heavy visual effect that makes mobile performance unacceptable.

Recompose sections where necessary rather than simply stacking every desktop element.

The mobile experience must feel intentional and premium in its own right.

# PHASE 7 — IMAGE AND ASSET STRATEGY

Visual assets are a major part of the final quality.

First inspect the supplied presentation and project directory for existing:

- Logos.
- Product screenshots.
- Project images.
- Company photography.
- Illustrations.
- Brand assets.
- Supporting documents.

Reuse authentic company assets when appropriate.

If additional imagery is required, select assets that fit the actual industry and art direction.

Prioritize:

1. Authentic company imagery.
2. Professionally sourced imagery with appropriate usage rights.
3. Custom illustrations or visual compositions.
4. Generated imagery when it genuinely adds value and can be used appropriately.

Do not use random stock photos of smiling businesspeople.

Do not display unrelated imagery just because it looks attractive.

Use optimized image formats, appropriate dimensions, responsive image delivery, and lazy loading where appropriate.

Do not lazy-load the primary above-the-fold image if doing so would unnecessarily delay the main visual.

If external image URLs are used, make sure they are reliable and suitable for production.

Avoid relying on fragile third-party image URLs or development-only asset services.

If a required image is unavailable, use a deliberate, visually coherent fallback and document the missing asset.

# PHASE 8 — CONTENT AND BRAND VOICE

The website's copy is as important as its design.

Use the language most appropriate to the company presentation and intended customers.

If the presentation is in French, default to professional, natural French unless the source indicates otherwise.

If it is in English, use polished, natural English.

If multilingual content is genuinely required, design the site to support it cleanly.

Write content that is:

- Specific.
- Clear.
- Credible.
- Concise.
- Persuasive without being exaggerated.
- Consistent with the company's real positioning.

Avoid lorem ipsum, repetitive slogans, vague marketing language, and invented claims.

Use correct grammar, accents, punctuation, and professional terminology.

Preserve important company names, service names, certifications, and product terminology.

Store repeated content in structured data or appropriate content files when that improves maintainability.

# PHASE 9 — ACCESSIBILITY, SEO, AND PERFORMANCE

Treat these as production requirements, not optional enhancements.

## Accessibility

Aim for WCAG 2.2 AA practices where applicable.

Implement:

- Semantic HTML.
- Logical heading hierarchy.
- Keyboard-accessible navigation.
- Visible focus indicators.
- Accessible form labels.
- Appropriate alternative text.
- Sufficient color contrast.
- Reduced-motion support.
- Appropriate ARIA only when necessary.
- Correct handling of menus, dialogs, and interactive components.

Never use color alone to communicate essential information.

## SEO

Implement:

- Appropriate page titles.
- Meta descriptions.
- Canonical URLs when a real production domain is known.
- Open Graph metadata.
- Appropriate social-sharing metadata.
- Semantic content structure.
- Descriptive headings.
- Meaningful internal links.
- A favicon using supplied branding where possible.
- A sitemap and robots.txt when appropriate to the selected framework and deployment.

Use structured data only when the information is accurate and the schema is appropriate.

Do not invent a production domain.

## Performance

Target excellent real-world Core Web Vitals.

Use these as performance objectives, not fabricated test results:

- LCP: 2.5 seconds or less.
- INP: 200 milliseconds or less.
- CLS: 0.1 or less.

Reduce unnecessary JavaScript.

Optimize images and fonts.

Avoid blocking the main thread with elaborate animations.

Load nonessential resources only when needed.

Use static generation, server rendering, code splitting, or lazy loading where appropriate.

Avoid adding a large library to implement an effect that could be achieved with a few lines of CSS.

# PHASE 10 — IMPLEMENTATION RULES

You are expected to execute the implementation, not merely describe what should be done.

Follow this sequence:

1. Inspect the repository and presentation.
2. Determine the brand strategy and visual direction.
3. Review the existing stack and identify constraints.
4. Establish the design system.
5. Implement the complete website.
6. Run the application.
7. Inspect the actual rendered result.
8. Test responsive layouts.
9. Fix visual defects and interaction problems.
10. Run the available build, lint, type-check, and tests.
11. Correct errors and regressions.
12. Provide a concise delivery report.

Do not stop after generating a plan, mockup, or initial hero section.

Do not ask me to implement the components myself.

Make reasonable decisions independently and proceed.

Ask a question only when a genuinely blocking issue cannot be resolved from the presentation, repository, or reasonable assumptions.

## Repository safety

Before changing anything:

- Inspect the existing project structure.
- Read the package manifest and relevant configuration.
- Identify the existing framework and conventions.
- Preserve useful code and assets.
- Avoid destructive commands.
- Never overwrite important user work without understanding its purpose.
- Avoid changing unrelated application behavior.
- Do not expose secrets or environment variables.

If the repository contains an existing website, improve it in place whenever practical instead of unnecessarily rebuilding everything.

## Code quality

Use:

- Clear component boundaries.
- Descriptive names.
- Strong typing.
- Reusable design primitives.
- Consistent formatting.
- Predictable responsive behavior.
- Maintainable CSS.
- Error handling where needed.

Avoid:

- Giant monolithic components.
- Duplicated markup.
- Unnecessary abstractions.
- Arbitrary magic numbers throughout the code.
- Unused dependencies.
- Fake interactions.
- Placeholder links.
- Broken imports.
- Unhandled console errors.

Do not disable linting or type checks just to make the build pass.

Do not claim that a feature works unless you have verified it to the extent possible.
